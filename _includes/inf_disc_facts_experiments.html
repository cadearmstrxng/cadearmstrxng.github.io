Experiment 1: Crosswalk Simulation

This experiment involves $N = 2$ pedestrians navigating a crosswalk at an intersection on the UT Austin campus. The interesection is unique in that it becomes safe for all pedestrian crossing simultaneously, allowing students to cross diagonally and interact with one another.

Experimental Setup

We simulate two short-sighted ($\gamma < 1$) delivery robots who begin at the top right and left corners, respectively, and wish to reach their goals at the corner's diagonal to them while minimizing their control effort. As such, we can model robot
$i$'s cost as: $$ \begin{align} J_{\text{robot}}^i = \sum_{t=0}^T (\gamma^i)^t [w_\text{goal}^i ||p_t^i - \theta_\text{goal}^i||_2^2 + w_\text{ctrl}^i ||u_t^i||_2^2]. \end{align} $$ where $p_t^i$ is agent $i$'s position at time $t$, $w_\text{goal}^i$ and $w_\text{ctrl}^i$ are cost weight terms for the goal and control cost, and $\theta_\text{goal}^i$ are parameters that define agent $i$'s goal position.

We model the robots as point masses with bounds on the velocity and control inputs, assume a time discretization of $\Delta = 0.1$, and play a game over $T = 25$ time steps. Each agent's dynamics are modeled with a standard double integrator system, with the game state $x_t = [x_t^{1,\top},x_t^{1,\top}]^\top$. We additionally include collision avoidance inequality constraints to each agent such that the agents stay a certain distance $d_\text{min}$ away from each other.

Results

This experiment was mostly used to test the feasibility of the proposed method and to understand the difficulty of the problem. To this end, by simulating the trajectories we wanted to recover via a known game formulation, we should be able to recover the exact parameters of the game when noise is not a factor. However, we are only interested in cases where noise is present, so there should be some slight error in the recovered trajectories.

In our initial attempts in this experiment, we found that our algorithm was able to infer the symmetry of the problem, but the recovered discount factors were very dependent on the initial guess. To isolate the root cause of this issue, we plotted a heatmap of $\mathcal{P}$ with respect to the discount factors in both the fully observable and partially observable cases, where the other parameters are fixed to their ground truth values.

The heatmap shows that there is a diagonally-shaped low-cost region when the discount factors are greater than ~$0.5$. This result shows that the game's equilibrium is relatively insensitive to the agents' absolute discount factors as long as they are similar. This motivated us to introduce a regularization scheme to Problem 1 to encourage better solver performance. To this end, we added the term $c_\gamma ||1-\gamma||_2^2$ to the inverse problem. This term encodes the belief that agents are more farsighted. It should be noted that we did not use this regularization in any other experiments.

Quick check: Suppose we wanted to instead encode the belief that agents are more short-sighted. What term would we add to the inverse problem?
Explanation: The minimum of $||\gamma||_2^2$ is achieved when $\gamma = 0$. Since we are wanting to minimize the inverse problem, by minimizing $||\gamma||_2^2$, we are encouraging the solver to find a $\gamma$ that is close to 0.

With the regularization scheme in place, we were able to turn our attention to the trajectory recovery results. We present results in both fully observable and partially observable settings with $c_\gamma = 10^{-3}$. The two graphs below show the mean trajectory estimation error as a function of the observation noise standard deviation $\sigma$.

The results show that our method substantially outperforms the baseline method in both cases. Interestingly, we can see that the our method's mean error is only marginally sensitive to noise for both methods. Rather, the most noticeable effect of noise is an increase in the standard deviation of the errors. With these results, we conclude that our foresighted formulation can better predict the behavior of short-sighted agents in comparison to a state-of-the-art baseline.

Experiment 2: InD Intersection

Our second experiment employs the inD Dataset, which contains real-world data of naturalistic road user trajectories at German intersections that were collected using drones. The intersection we look at in this experiment (shown below) is found in Aachen, Germany.

This experiment involves $N = 4$ agents navigating this intersection. Two of the agents are pedestrians crossing a crosswalk, one is a car attempting to turn while the pedestrians are crossing, and the final agent is a car attempting to go straight while behind the turning car.

Experimental Setup

The setup for our model in this example is very similar to that of the crosswalk simulation experiment. To begin, all agents have the previously mentioned collision avoidance inequality constraints. Additionally, we add inequality constraints so that the car agents are constrained to the road boundaries. We add similar constraints for the pedestrians so that they stay on the sidewalk.

We model the pedestrians exactly as we modeled the robots in the crosswalk simulation experiment. Meanwhile, the cars' physical dynamics are modeled as bicycles with bounds on the control inputs. Explicitly, car agent $i$'s state is $x_t^i = [p_{x,t}^i, p_{y,t}^i, v_{t}^i, \psi_{t}^i]^\top$, where $p_{x,t}^i$ and $p_{y,t}^i$ are the $x$ and $y$ coordinates of the car's position at time $t$, $v_{t}^i$ is the car's velocity, and $\psi_{t}^i$ is the car's heading angle. The dynamics are given by: $$ \begin{align*} {x}_{t+1}^i = [ p_{x,t}^i,~ p_{y,t}^i,~ v_{t}^i,~ \psi_{t}^i ]^\top+ \Delta [ v^i_t \cos(\psi_t^i),~ v^i_t \sin(\psi_t^i),~ a_t^i,~ v_t^i \tan(\phi_t^i)/l ]^\top \end{align*} $$ with control input $u_t^i = [a_t^i, \phi_t^i]^\top$, where $a_t^i$ is the car's acceleration and $\phi_t^i$ is the car's steering angle. Additionally, $l$ is the wheelbase of the car.

Depending on whether the $i^{th}$ agent is a pedestrian or a car, we model their cost slightly differently. The two pedestrian agents use the same cost as the robots in the previous crosswalk simulation experiment. The car agents have an additional cost term that minimize the distance to their lane centers. We model the $i^{th}$ car agent's lane center as a set, $\mathcal{C}^i = \{(c_{x,t}^i,c_{y,t}^i)\}_{t = 0}^T$, generated from a polynomial curve fit to lane center points. With this in hand, we can model the $i^{th}$ car agent's cost as: $$ \begin{align} J_{\text{car}}^i = J_{\text{robot}}^i + \sum_{t=0}^T (\gamma^i)^t [w_\text{lane}^i ||p_t^i - c_t^i||_2^2. \end{align} $$ where $w_\text{lane}^i$ weights the lane center cost term and $c_t^i$ is the $t^{th}$ element of set $\mathcal{C}^i$. The original inD dataset interaction we use takes place over $162$ frames at $25$ frames per second. To help with computational efficiency, we downsample the data by a factor of $6$, resulting in a game horizon of $T = 28$.

Results

The purpose of this experiment is to demonstrate that our foresighted model better predicts real-world human behavior in comparison to a state-of-the-art baseline. To this end, we focus this experiment on fitting our model to the data collected from the interaction shown in the figure above and compare the results to the baseline.

In the above figure, we present a representative trial of our method and the baseline with the real-world data from the inD dataset. The figure shows the initial positions, the percieved and inferred goals, the observations and recovered trajectories up to a time $t = 15$, and the planned trajectories for all four agents at $t = 15$ for the baseline and our method. This visualization shows a better recovery of the ground-truth goal position than the baseline and a trajectory recovery that is closer to the observations in L$_2$ distance.

The two graphs below show the mean trajectory estimation error as a function of the observation noise standard deviation $\sigma$ for the fully observable and partially observable cases.

On average, our method consistently shows a significant improvement on the baseline in both the fully observable and partially observable cases. However, both methods seem to be relatively insensitive to increases in noise. Also, see that the noiseless-observation trajectory error is non-zero? We attribute that to model mismatch, referring to the differences between actual human behavior and the model's recreations of it. We hypothesize two dominate sources of model mismatch:

  1. Our model's open-loop information structure, which was chosen for tractability. We hypothesize that closed-loop information structures is more representative of human decision-making processes.
  2. The expressiveness of our cost structure is likely limited in its ability to capture the true human behavior. More complex cost structures, such as those encoded by neural networks, may be able to capture more complex human behavior.

These results then show that our foresighted model can better predict real-world human behavior in comparison to a state-of-the-art baseline, making it a useful tool that can be used to enable safer and more efficient interactions between robots and humans!
